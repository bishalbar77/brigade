Brigade — greybox wireframes

Structure only. No palette, no type, no visual direction — that is frontend-design's job.

How to read these

Grey blocks are content. Dashed regions are containers, labelled at the corner. Hatched blocks marked SIGNATURE are where the runway countdown goes — deliberately undesigned, because that form is the one thing frontend-design should invent.

These are deliberately ugly. System monospace, greyscale, no spacing polish. That is the point: a wireframe that looks designed anchors the designer to whatever it accidentally suggested. The aesthetics rules apply to the product, not to this file.

Guest screens are framed at 375px (smallest real phone). Ops screens are wide — the KDS is a wall-mounted screen read from ~2m, which is why its type is oversized here.

Right-hand notes carry intent and edge cases, not styling. Where a note says "see docs/features/x.md" the full rules live there.

Intent

  • The hero must prove the product, not describe it. A live availability figure pulled from real data beats any headline claim. If the hero is just big type + a gradient, we've built the template answer.
  • This page exists for judges as much as diners. First impression for US1 (Bronze). It has to communicate the thesis in about five seconds.
  • The live strip doubles as the demo opener — point at a real number and say "that came from the pantry."
  • Show a genuinely 86'd dish in the strip. Honesty about scarcity is the pitch.

Edge cases

  • Outside service hours → strip shows portions but no countdown. A landing page predicting an 86 at 04:00 destroys trust in the number.
  • Fewer than 3 dishes with data → collapse the strip rather than pad it with placeholders.

Intent

Why this screen matters most

  • This is where Brigade stops being a Toast clone. Toast computes this number and shows it only to staff. Putting it in front of the person choosing is the whole idea.
  • Four visual states map to the runway bands: plenty (no badge) → low → critical → 86.

Rules

  • Ranking, not hiding. Near-86 dishes sink and gain a badge but stay orderable. Hiding an available dish is a lie, and a guest who came for the branzino must still find it.
  • Steering sorts within a category only. Reordering across starters and mains makes a menu unusable.
  • No dish with an empty BOM shows as unavailable — no recipe means unlimited. A half-configured menu must not read as a closed kitchen.
  • Never leak cost or margin. Margin drives the sort; the guest sees only the order.
  • Guests are not told why something is 86'd. Ingredient stock is staff information.

The hard part

  • Two guests both see "1 left" and tap simultaneously. A read-then-write in app code lets both through and drives stock negative.

Intent

  • A guest who can see progress stops asking the server, which removes a trip for the server too. One feature, both sides of the PS "delayed communication" bullet.
  • Per-item, not per-order. An order-level status cannot express "starter away, main still cooking" — which is the entire job of the pass.

Rules

  • Estimates are ranges. A wrong precise number costs more trust than an honest range. If kitchen load can't be computed, suppress rather than show 0.
  • RLS restricts to the order's owner — changing the URL to another order returns nothing, enforced in the database.
  • Must self-heal: refetch on reconnect and on tab focus. Guests go through tunnels and lifts; a screen stuck on stale state looks broken.

Intent

  • The "smart" in smart reservations is that the quote comes from this restaurant's actual seated→closed durations, bucketed by party size, not from a host's guess.
  • Letting the guest leave and be called back is the real fix for "long waiting times for tables."

Rules

  • Overbooking refused at the API. The picker is convenience; capacity is enforced server-side.
  • Cold start (<10 historical turns for a bucket) → configured default, and don't present it as data-driven.
  • Party size bucketed (1–2, 3–4, 5–6, 7+). A 2-top and 4-top turn differently; 3 vs 4 is noise.
  • No-show grace window before releasing a held table — instant release punishes traffic, never releasing blocks the table all night.
  • One active queue entry per guest, to stop queue-stuffing.

Rules

Environment is the brief

  • Rahul is standing, hot, hands busy, reading from ~2m through glare on a grease-filmed screen. No mouse, no hover, no small text, no drag-and-drop.
  • Ticket age is the critical number. A docket sitting 23 minutes must be unmissable without anyone watching the screen.

  • Must survive an 8-hour session without reload — no subscription leak, no unbounded DOM. Completed dockets leave the board.
  • Realtime drop → reconnect and refetch. A KDS showing stale tickets is actively dangerous.

This is the product's whole claim

  • Existing POS says a dish is out. This says when it will be, while there's still time to prep more, adjust, or brief the floor.
  • Step 5 of the demo script: "this is the part no POS does."

Rules that protect trust in the number

  • Outside service hours, suppress predictions. Velocity is 0, so runway is undefined. A board predicting an 86 at 04:00 destroys trust in everything else on screen.
  • Cold start is marked, not fabricated. <3 samples → show portions, say "not enough history."
  • portions ≤ 3 forces critical regardless of rate — at low counts the ratio is noisy and "3 left" matters on its own.
  • Always name the binding ingredient. That's the difference between information and action.
  • Countdown animates on change, then rests. A permanently moving element in a kitchen is noise.
  • Copy is approximate ("~20:40") — never false precision.

Degradation, in order

  • sell rate → inline stock adjust → animated countdown. Never drop the predicted 86 time.

Why a ledger, not a number

  • There is no "just edit the stock" affordance. That's how inventory systems become fiction. Every change carries a reason and an actor.
  • A stock count writes a correction movement for the difference — it does not overwrite the balance. The gap between counted and expected is the variance signal; overwriting destroys the data the Bonus feature needs.
  • Stock is only ever mutated by place_order() or adjust_stock(). Never a bare UPDATE. The reconciliation query in docs/08-runbook.md proves it.

Rules

  • Reorder suggestions are capped by shelf life — otherwise a naive system generates the very waste it was bought to prevent.
  • Variance is framed "investigate," never "someone stole this." Over-portioning, bad yields and theft look identical in this data.
  • Costs are owner/manager only, enforced by column grants — never reachable from a guest payload.

Why this screen is not cuttable

  • Without a BOM editor the differentiator can't be configured or demoed. This is the screen that turns "3 kg of chicken" into "you can serve 6 of these."

Bar to clear

  • Every chart must answer a question an owner actually has — what lost money, what to order, where stock is going — not plot revenue over time because it's easy.
  • The matrix is the chart that makes an owner change something on Monday. It's the most credible Gold/Platinum artifact and it's computed from data the product already has.

Rules

  • Historical cost, not current. Margin uses cost as at time of sale — if a supplier raises a price today, last month's profit must not change, or the numbers are unauditable.
  • No realtime here. A matrix that reshuffles while you read it is worse, not better.
  • Archived dishes still appear in historical ranges — they sold in that period.
  • Owner/manager only. These payloads contain cost and margin.

Depends on

  • The 6-week seed script. Without it every panel here is empty and the forecast has no signal. That's why it's day-1 core work, not scaffolding.

What's deliberately absent

Staff management (cut-line 1), split billing (cut-line 2), supplier auto-ordering (3), and dish modifiers (5) have no wireframe. They're documented in docs/features/ with their cut rationale. Wireframing what's most likely to be dropped invites building it.

No palette, no typefaces, no spacing system. Those are free axes for frontend-design, with one constraint: don't spend them on the three known AI-design defaults. Flagged risk — a thermal-docket aesthetic is right for the KDS but sits one step from the "broadsheet, hairline rules, zero radius" cluster. Keep the docket a component, not the whole page.
